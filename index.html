A field survey of 55 respondents from a college and its surrounding community, measuring not just what people know about online safety, but whether that knowledge shows up in what they actually do.

01 Social Media Safety

Six habits that keep an account safe

Safety on social media isn't one setting — it's a set of small, repeatable habits: how you lock your account, how you verify who's messaging you, and how you react in the few seconds before you tap something you shouldn't.

Strong passwords

A strong password is long, unique to that one account, and not reused anywhere else — so if one site is breached, the rest of your accounts stay safe. A password manager makes this painless: it remembers dozens of unique passwords so you don't have to.

54.5% already use a strong, unique password

Two-factor authentication

2FA adds a second check after your password — a code sent to your phone or generated by an app — so a stolen password alone can't get someone into your account. It's one of the single most effective account protections available, and it takes about a minute to turn on.

Just 41.8% have 2FA on — the weakest habit this survey measured

Privacy settings

Privacy settings decide who can see your posts, find your profile, or send you a request. They're worth a few minutes every so often — platforms change their defaults more often than most people check them.

65.5% keep their main account private — but only 23.6% review settings regularly

Suspicious links

Unexpected links — even from people you know — are one of the easiest ways an account gets taken over. The safe move is to pause: check where a link actually leads, and verify with the sender through another channel before tapping.

81.8% have already received a suspicious link or request

Fake accounts

Fake and impersonation accounts often show a thin history, borrowed photos, and a rush to connect or ask for something. A quick check of someone's profile before accepting a request catches most of them.

Named the #1 perceived risk — by 72.7% of respondents

Phishing & scams

Phishing messages borrow urgency and trust — a "your account will be locked," a too-good prize, a friend suddenly asking for money. Slowing down for ten seconds is usually enough to spot the mismatch.

Only 47.3% feel confident (4 or 5 out of 5) spotting one
02 Digital Footprint

The trail you leave without noticing

Every post, comment, like and profile update adds to a record that outlives the moment you made it. A digital footprint isn't one big decision — it's the sum of many small, ordinary ones.

What is a digital footprint?

It's the collection of traces your online activity leaves behind — posts, comments, photos, likes, public profiles and interactions — that together form a lasting, searchable record of your interests, opinions and relationships.

60.0% say they clearly understand the term

What you post online

Location tags, school or workplace names, and daily routines are small details on their own, but combined they can sketch a surprisingly complete picture of where someone is and when. Most respondents already hold back on this.

74.5% rarely or never share such details publicly

Future education & employment impact

Admissions officers and hiring managers routinely look up public profiles. A post that felt harmless at 16 can resurface at a scholarship interview or a job application years later.

80.0% believe posts can affect future opportunities

Responsible posting

The simplest habit is also the most effective: pause and ask whether this would still feel fine if a teacher, employer or future you saw it. Right now that pause isn't yet automatic for most respondents.

Only 20% say they always reflect before posting

04 Research Methodology

How the survey was built and read

A field project is only as trustworthy as the process behind it. Here's exactly how the questionnaire was designed, who it reached, and how the raw responses became the charts above.

01

Research design

A descriptive survey design: a structured questionnaire administered once to each respondent, producing cross-sectional data analysed through frequencies, percentages, and a direct side-by-side comparison of paired awareness and practice indicators.

02

Questionnaire design

21 items grouped into six thematic blocks (A–F, shown as the section headers above). Most use single-choice answers; three use checkbox-style multiple selection; one is a five-point confidence scale; one is open-ended. The instrument was pilot-tested with 5 respondents, which led to two questions being reworded for clarity.

03

Population, sample & sampling technique

Active social-media users in a college and its surrounding community, sampled by convenience — the standard approach for a time-bound field project where respondents must be reachable in person or through college networks. 58 questionnaires were distributed and 55 usable responses came back (3 incomplete returns were excluded), a 94.8% response rate. The realised sample skews toward college students aged 18–24; this is reported transparently rather than presented as representative of a wider population.

04

Data collection

Primary data were collected between 20 August To 3 September 2026, via a Google Form administered two ways: in person on campus and in the surrounding neighbourhood, and through a shared link circulated to community contacts. Every respondent was told the study's purpose and that participation was voluntary and anonymous before starting. Average completion time was 6–8 minutes.

05

Data processing

Responses were exported to a spreadsheet, checked for completeness, and coded. Percentages for single-choice items use 55 as the base. For the three multiple-selection items (platforms, purpose, perceived risks), each option is counted separately, so those totals exceed 55 — they represent selections, not unique respondents. Percentages are rounded to one decimal place, so some columns don't sum to exactly 100.0%.

06

Ethics & limitations

No names, passwords, account handles, phone numbers or addresses were collected — responses are identified only by a serial code. Participation was voluntary with verbal consent, and respondents under 18 were surveyed only with a teacher or parent present. Honestly stated, the study also has real limits: the convenience sample is small and skews toward college-age respondents, all data is self-reported (so protective practice may be somewhat overstated), and the design is cross-sectional — it captures one point in time, not a change over time.

05 Safety Guide

Four moments, four habits

Most social-media risk comes down to a handful of ordinary moments. Here's what to do in each one — short enough to actually remember.

Before You Click

  1. Stop before tapping an unexpected link — even one from someone you know.
  2. Preview where it actually leads, rather than trusting the link text alone.
  3. Verify with the sender through a different channel if anything feels off.
  4. Report and delete it if it still looks suspicious — don't just ignore it and move on.

Before You Post

  1. Ask whether this would still feel fine if a teacher, employer, or future you saw it.
  2. Leave out live location, school or workplace names, and daily routines where you can.
  3. Remember that screenshots and shares outlast the original post.
  4. When in doubt, save it as a draft and revisit it tomorrow.

Protect Your Account

  1. Use a long, unique password for every account — a password manager makes this painless.
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it's offered.
  3. Set your main account to private and review who can see it every few months.
  4. Check which apps and devices still have access, and remove ones you don't recognise.

If Something Goes Wrong

  1. Block the account first — you can always undo it later.
  2. Report it through the platform's built-in reporting tool.
  3. In India, serious cases can also go to the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or the 1930 helpline.
  4. Tell someone you trust, especially if it involves harassment or impersonation.
  5. Save screenshots as evidence before the content can be deleted or edited.

Not an awareness problem — a procedural one

Respondents who could describe the danger of a fake profile in detail had often never opened their own privacy settings. That's encouraging, in a way: procedural gaps close faster than attitudes change, and 74.5% of respondents said they'd attend a session to close it.

Project objectives

  • Assess the pattern and intensity of social-media usage — time spent, platforms, and purposes of use.
  • Examine account-security practices: privacy, password strength, and two-factor authentication.
  • Measure awareness of the digital footprint and its possible effect on future education and employment.
  • Identify the risks respondents perceive as most serious and how they respond to suspicious contact.
  • Determine how far awareness of risk has translated into changed behaviour — the awareness-to-action gap.
  • Develop practical, community-level recommendations for safer social-media use.
